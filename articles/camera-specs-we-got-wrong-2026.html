The camera specs we got wrong (and why it happens)

We checked our specs against the manufacturers. The manufacturers won. Often.

By Compare Camera Specs · Published 22 November 2026

When we went back to the manufacturers' own spec pages, we found a Canon EOS R1 listed 455 g too heavy, four cameras listed without the viewfinder they actually have, and a camera wearing its sibling's dimensions.

Most wrong specs aren't typos. They're numbers from the wrong place: a different mount, a secondhand source, an older model, or nowhere at all. Here's what we found, and why each one happened.

1. Same lens, many mounts

Sigma makes most of its lenses for several camera systems. Of the 56 current Sigma lenses in our database, 44 are sold for more than one mount, and 34 of those weigh differently from one mount to the next.

Sigma's page for the 56mm f/1.4 lists 7 mounts, from 256 g on Micro Four Thirds to 295 g on Nikon Z. Each mount has its own diameter and sits a different distance from the sensor, so the barrel changes. Same glass, different lens.

That's what caught us. 19 of the 22 Sigma L-Mount lenses we checked were carrying their Sony E-mount dimensions, read from the wrong row of Sigma's spec table. L-Mount sits 2 mm further from the sensor than Sony E (20 mm vs 18 mm), so L-Mount barrels are usually about 2 mm shorter. Browse the corrected L-Mount lenses, or every Nikon Z lens.

2. Wikipedia isn't the manufacturer

When we first added Olympus, most of its cameras cited Wikipedia. Then we checked the ones we could against Olympus's own spec sheets. Some of the oldest still rely on Wikipedia.

The E-M5 Mark II's battery life, as we'd taken it from Wikipedia, was 580 shots. Olympus says 310, or 750 in power-saving mode. 580 matches neither. The PEN-F was 7 g off and the E-M10 Mark II 9 g.

And the E-PL5's 279 g wasn't wrong, just body only. With battery and card, the way we list every camera, it's 325 g. Wikipedia is only as good as the reviews and spec sites it cites, and those often copy each other.

3. When the manufacturer's page disappears

Manufacturers quietly delete old spec pages. For 10 older Fujifilm cameras, including the original X100 and X-Pro1, the spec pages now redirect to Fujifilm's home page. Only the owner's manuals are left.

Olympus is worse: for 10 older cameras we found no manufacturer spec page at all. For others, the archive is a picture of the old page, which we had to run through text recognition, in Japanese.

Leica's PDFs vanish too: 12 of the camera spec sheets we'd cited, and 17 of 48 lens sheets, now return errors. Once the manufacturer's page is gone, secondhand copies are often the easiest record to find, mistakes and all.

4. The phantom 455 grams

We had Canon's flagship EOS R1 at 1,570 g. Canon says 1,115 g with battery and card. The gap alone weighs more than an entire EOS R50.

Where did 1,570 g come from? We can't say. It arrived with our first batch of Canon data in June 2026, and back then we didn't save our citations while doing the research. The rest of that entry was off too: the size, the megapixels and the screen.

That's the real lesson. A number without a source can't be checked, so nobody ever fixes it. Now, when we check a camera or lens, we save links to the sources we used. See the corrected figures in the EOS R1 vs EOS R3 comparison.

5. Hand-me-down specs

A classic: a new model carrying its predecessor's numbers. The EOS R50 V had the EOS R50's dimensions and weight. In reality it's 45.2 mm deep, not 68.8 mm.

Fujifilm's XF 16-55mm f/2.8 II carried the 2015 original's specs: 655 g instead of 410 g. The OM-5 II had the OM-5's depth, and Sony's FE 16-35mm GM II had the first GM's length (111.5 mm, not 121.6 mm).

Why? The quick way to add a new model is to start from the old one and change what the launch news mentions. Launch news is about sensors and autofocus, not millimeters, so the old size and weight ride along. Our own note on the Olympus E-PL8 said it outright: dimensions "assumed similar to e-pl7". They weren't.

See how different the R50 V really is in the EOS R50 V vs EOS R50 size comparison, or the OM-5 II vs OM-5.

6. The viewfinders that vanished

Four cameras were listed with no viewfinder: Canon's EOS R50 and EOS R100, and Fujifilm's X-T100 and X-T200. All four have an electronic viewfinder, as Canon and Fujifilm list.

Why? In a spec table, "we don't know" and "it doesn't have one" look exactly the same: an empty cell. All four are entry-level bodies, where a missing viewfinder sounds believable, so nobody questioned the blank.

The R50 also has a near-twin that really has no viewfinder: the EOS R50 V. Our R50 V had the R50's size, and our R50 had the R50 V's missing viewfinder. It looks like the two got muddled.

The X-T100 was also 54 g light: Fujifilm says 448 g, not 394. Compare the EOS R50 and EOS R100, or the X-T100 and X-T200.

7. Weather sealing, both ways

We had the Fujifilm X-T50 down as weather-sealed. Fujifilm never says so, and reviewers list the lack of sealing as a downside.

The likely culprit is its name. The X-T50 sounds like a smaller X-T5, and the X-T5 has 56 weather-sealed points. Family resemblance isn't a spec.

The Canon EOS R8 shows how messy it gets. Canon UK lists "weather-resistant construction"; Canon Japan's page makes no such claim. When a manufacturer disagrees with itself, we leave it unsealed until another Canon source settles it.

Why is this so easy to get wrong? There's no standard. Canon UK says "weather-resistant", Sigma says "dust and splash resistant", and on its 100-400mm that means a seal at the mount only. Many manufacturers just say nothing. Silence isn't a no, but it isn't a yes either. We now mark a camera or lens sealed only when its manufacturer says so. See the X-T50 vs X100V and EOS R8 vs EOS R10.

8. Hood on, zoomed out

Some numbers aren't wrong, just measured differently. Leica's Summilux-M 28mm f/1.4 was 81 mm long with its hood on; without it it's 67 mm.

Fujifilm's XF 55-200mm was listed at 175 mm, its length zoomed all the way out. At rest it's 118 mm.

How to spot a bad spec

  • Two models, same numbers? Suspicious. New bodies rarely match old ones to the millimeter.
  • Check what's included. Battery and card? Hood? Zoomed in or out? Which mount?
  • Go to the manufacturer. If the manufacturer doesn't claim it (weather sealing, especially), don't believe it.
  • No source, no trust. A figure nobody can trace is a guess.
