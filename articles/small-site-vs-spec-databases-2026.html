Why a small site can get camera specs more right than a big database

We can't out-list them. We can out-check them.

By Compare Camera Specs · Published 21 November 2026

A big spec database lists more cameras than anyone can check by hand, so it copies. We list fewer, and 93% of our 1,477 cameras and lenses link straight to the maker's own spec page. Being small is what makes that possible.

That's not a dig at the big sites. It's a trade-off: they win on coverage, we try to win on checking. And because a careful site can still be wrong, we publish the rules we check by.

Why big spec databases drift

Size is the problem, not care. Three things go wrong at scale.

Copies of copies. Most stores list specs from the same distributor feed, and databases often fill their gaps from stores and from each other. One typo can end up on a hundred pages that all "agree".

One entry for many mounts. A lens sold for five mounts is five products, with different weights and lengths. When we checked one large lens database this year, its spec tables matched the makers, yet it listed 6 lenses for Canon RF that their makers don't sell for RF.

Mixed rules. Makers quote the same thing in different ways, and a database with one "battery life" box has to pick. Fujifilm rates the X100VI at 310 shots with the electronic viewfinder and 450 with the optical one. OM System rates the OM-5 Mark II at 6.5 stops of stabilization on its own and 7.5 with a matching lens. Neither number is wrong. Comparing one camera's best case with another's normal case is.

How much of our data can you trace to the maker?

Here's the share of each brand's cameras that cite the maker's own spec, across 45 maker websites in all.

Canon, Nikon, Panasonic, Sigma and Sony are fully traced. Every camera we can't yet trace is discontinued, the newest from 2019. Their maker pages are gone, which is exactly where a secondhand number is most tempting.

Where we're still weaker

We don't weigh anything. We have no lab, so when a maker is wrong, we're wrong with it. Makers' own pages sometimes copy a figure from one product to another.

Old cameras are hard. When a maker deletes a page, we're left with manuals and archives, and some of those never made it online.

We've made our own mistakes. We wrote up the specs we got wrong, from a Canon flagship listed far too heavy to cameras missing their viewfinders.

Coverage. A big database will have the camera you're looking for. We might not: we cover mirrorless cameras from 8 brands and their lenses.

Why we publish the rules

Checking is only half of it. If you can't see how a number was chosen, you can't tell a careful site from a confident one.

So our methodology lists which sources we trust, which number we store when makers disagree, and every rule change with its date. A figure that breaks one of those rules is a bug, and you can report it on GitHub.
